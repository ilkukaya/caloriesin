## Introduction

Calorie counting is one of the most straightforward approaches to managing your weight. The concept is simple: track the energy you consume through food and drinks, and compare it to the energy your body burns. When you consistently eat fewer calories than you burn, you lose weight. When you eat more, you gain weight.

Despite its simplicity, many beginners find calorie counting confusing or overwhelming at first. This guide walks you through the entire process step by step, so you can start tracking with confidence and avoid the most common pitfalls.

## Step 1: Determine Your Daily Calorie Target

Before you start counting, you need to know how many calories you should aim for each day. Your target depends on your goal:

- **Weight loss:** Eat fewer calories than your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE), typically 500 calories less for a loss of about 0.5 kg (1 lb) per week.
- **Weight maintenance:** Eat roughly the same number of calories as your TDEE.
- **Weight gain or muscle building:** Eat more calories than your TDEE, typically 250 to 500 extra per day.

You can estimate your TDEE using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation or by using our [Calorie Calculator](/tools/calorie-calculator), which does the math automatically based on your age, sex, height, weight, and activity level.

## Step 2: Get a Food Scale

A kitchen food scale is the single most important tool for accurate calorie counting. Estimating portion sizes by eye is notoriously unreliable — studies show that most people underestimate their portions by 20 to 50%.

A basic digital food scale costs very little and takes the guesswork out of measuring. Weigh your food in grams whenever possible, as this is the most precise method.

## Step 3: Learn to Read Nutrition Labels

Every packaged food includes a nutrition label with calorie information. Pay close attention to:

- **Serving size.** The calories listed are per serving, not per package. A bag of chips might list 150 calories per serving but contain three servings.
- **Calories per 100g.** This makes it easy to compare different products and calculate calories for any portion size.
- **Total calories.** Multiply the calories per serving by the number of servings you actually eat.

For fresh foods without labels — like fruits, vegetables, and meats — you can look up calorie values on CaloriesIn or in a food tracking app.

## Step 4: Track Everything You Eat and Drink

Consistency is key. Log every meal, snack, and beverage throughout the day. This includes:

- Cooking oils and butter used in preparation
- Sauces, dressings, and condiments
- Beverages including milk in coffee, juice, and alcohol
- Small bites and tastes while cooking

These "invisible" calories are where most people go wrong. A splash of olive oil here and a handful of nuts there can easily add 300 to 500 untracked calories to your day.

## Step 5: Use a Tracking App or Journal

Choose a method that works for your lifestyle:

- **Smartphone apps** offer large food databases, barcode scanning, and automatic calculations. They are the most popular option for good reason.
- **A simple notebook** works if you prefer a low-tech approach. Write down each food, its weight, and its calorie count.
- **A spreadsheet** gives you flexibility and a record you can analyze over time.

The best method is whichever one you will actually use consistently. Do not overcomplicate it.

## Step 6: Review and Adjust Weekly

Calorie counting is not a set-it-and-forget-it process. Weigh yourself once a week under consistent conditions — same time of day, same clothing — and track the trend over time.

- **If you are losing weight too quickly** (more than 1 kg per week), increase your calories by 200 to 300.
- **If you are not losing weight,** double-check your tracking accuracy and consider reducing your target by 100 to 200 calories.
- **If your weight fluctuates day to day,** do not panic. Water retention, sodium intake, and hormonal changes cause normal fluctuations of 1 to 2 kg.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### Not Tracking on Weekends

Many people track diligently Monday through Friday but stop on weekends. Two days of untracked overeating can easily wipe out a week's worth of calorie deficit.

### Forgetting Liquid Calories

A large glass of orange juice has about 110 calories. A latte with whole milk has about 190. Two glasses of wine add 250. These add up fast when untracked.

### Relying on Restaurant Calorie Estimates

Restaurant meals are difficult to track accurately because preparation methods vary widely. Portions are typically larger than home-cooked meals, and added butter, oil, and sauces often go unaccounted for. When eating out, estimate on the higher side.

### Being Too Restrictive

Setting your calories too low is unsustainable and often leads to binge eating. A moderate deficit of 500 calories is far more effective long term than an aggressive 1,000-calorie cut that lasts only a week.

### Obsessing Over Exact Numbers

Calorie counting does not need to be perfect to be effective. Aiming for accuracy within 100 to 200 calories of your target is realistic and sufficient for most goals. Do not let the pursuit of perfection become a source of stress.

## Tips for Long-Term Success

- **Meal prep** when possible. Planning meals in advance makes tracking easier and reduces impulsive food choices.
- **Build a library of go-to meals** with known calorie counts. This reduces the daily effort of looking everything up.
- **Be patient.** Meaningful body composition changes take weeks, not days. Trust the process and focus on the weekly trend.
- **Take breaks if needed.** If calorie counting starts to feel obsessive or stressful, step back and eat intuitively for a while. You can always return to tracking later.

Calorie counting is a skill that gets easier with practice. Most people find that after two to three weeks, they can estimate portions and calories fairly accurately without weighing every item. The initial effort pays off with lasting knowledge about the food you eat.
