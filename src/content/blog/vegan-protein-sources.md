## Introduction

One of the most common questions vegans face is "Where do you get your protein?" The answer is: from a wide variety of plant foods. While individual plant proteins tend to be lower in one or more essential amino acids compared to animal proteins, eating a varied plant-based diet easily covers all protein needs — including for athletes and bodybuilders.

The key is knowing which plant foods are richest in protein and how to combine them for optimal amino acid coverage. This guide covers the 20 best vegan protein sources, ranked by protein per 100g, with practical tips for building high-protein vegan meals.

## How Much Protein Do Vegans Need?

Vegans may benefit from aiming slightly higher than the standard protein recommendations because plant proteins generally have lower digestibility (80-90%) compared to animal proteins (95-100%):

- **Sedentary adults:** 1.0g per kg (vs. 0.8g for omnivores)
- **Recreational exercisers:** 1.4-1.8g per kg
- **Strength athletes:** 1.8-2.4g per kg
- **During fat loss:** 2.0-2.6g per kg

For a 70 kg vegan strength athlete, that means 126-168g of protein per day — absolutely achievable with strategic food choices.

## Complete vs. Incomplete Proteins

**Complete proteins** contain all nine essential amino acids in adequate amounts. Most animal proteins are complete, but several plant proteins are too:

- Soy (tofu, tempeh, edamame)
- [Quinoa](/quinoa)
- Hemp seeds
- Buckwheat
- Amaranth

**Incomplete proteins** are lower in one or more essential amino acids. Most grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds fall into this category. However, combining different incomplete proteins throughout the day provides all essential amino acids. You do not need to combine them in the same meal — eating a variety across the day is sufficient.

## The 20 Best Vegan Protein Sources

### 1. Seitan — 25g protein per 100g (126 calories)
Made from wheat gluten, seitan has more protein per calorie than almost any plant food. Its chewy, meat-like texture makes it popular in stir-fries, sandwiches, and grilled dishes. Note: not suitable for people with celiac disease or gluten sensitivity.

### 2. Tempeh — 19g protein per 100g (192 calories)
Fermented soybeans with a firm, nutty texture. Tempeh is a complete protein and the fermentation process makes it easier to digest. Slice and pan-fry, crumble into tacos, or marinate and bake.

### 3. [Lentils](/lentils) (cooked) — 9g protein per 100g (116 calories)
One of the most versatile and affordable plant proteins. A 200g serving delivers 18g of protein plus 16g of fiber. Red lentils cook in 15 minutes, making them perfect for weeknight meals.

### 4. Edamame — 11g protein per 100g (121 calories)
Young soybeans that are a complete protein. Eat them steamed with sea salt as a snack, or add to grain bowls and salads. A 200g serving provides 22g of protein.

### 5. Black Beans (cooked) — 9g protein per 100g (132 calories)
A staple in Latin American cuisine. Pair with [rice](/rice) for a complete amino acid profile. Excellent in burritos, soups, and salads.

### 6. Chickpeas (cooked) — 9g protein per 100g (164 calories)
The base of [hummus](/hummus) and a versatile ingredient in curries, salads, and roasted snacks. A cup of chickpeas provides about 15g of protein.

### 7. [Tofu](/tofu) (firm) — 8g protein per 100g (76 calories)
A complete soy protein with an incredible protein-to-calorie ratio. Press it, cube it, and bake, stir-fry, or scramble it. Firm and extra-firm varieties have the most protein.

### 8. Hemp Seeds — 31g protein per 100g (553 calories)
A complete protein with all essential amino acids. Calorie-dense, but a 30g serving (3 tablespoons) adds 10g of protein to smoothies, [oats](/oats), salads, and bowls.

### 9. Pumpkin Seeds — 19g protein per 100g (559 calories)
Rich in protein, magnesium, and zinc. Sprinkle on salads, yogurt alternatives, or eat as a snack. A 30g serving provides 6g of protein.

### 10. [Peanut Butter](/peanut-butter) — 25g protein per 100g (588 calories)
Calorie-dense but protein-rich. Two tablespoons (32g) provide 8g of protein. Choose natural varieties without added sugar or oil.

### 11. [Almonds](/almonds) — 21g protein per 100g (579 calories)
Among the highest-protein nuts. A 30g handful provides 6g of protein along with vitamin E, magnesium, and healthy fats.

### 12. [Quinoa](/quinoa) (cooked) — 4g protein per 100g (120 calories)
One of the few plant grains that is a complete protein. While the protein per serving is modest, quinoa serves as a superior alternative to [rice](/rice) when you are trying to maximize protein from every source.

### 13. [Oats](/oats) — 13g protein per 100g dry (389 calories)
Higher in protein than most grains. A 60g serving of oats provides about 8g of protein. Make overnight oats or oatmeal with soy milk and hemp seeds for a 20g+ protein breakfast.

### 14. Green Peas (cooked) — 5g protein per 100g (81 calories)
A surprisingly good protein source among vegetables. A 200g serving provides 10g of protein along with fiber and vitamin K.

### 15. Nutritional Yeast — 50g protein per 100g (325 calories)
A deactivated yeast with a cheesy, savory flavor. Just 2 tablespoons (16g) add 8g of protein to any dish. Popular sprinkled on popcorn, pasta, and roasted vegetables.

### 16. Soy Milk — 3.3g protein per 100ml (33 calories)
The only plant milk with protein comparable to cow's milk. A 250ml glass provides about 8g of protein. Choose fortified varieties for calcium and vitamin D.

### 17. Black-Eyed Peas (cooked) — 8g protein per 100g (116 calories)
A nutritious legume common in Southern and African cuisines. High in protein, fiber, and folate.

### 18. Spirulina — 57g protein per 100g (290 calories)
An algae with an extraordinary protein concentration. However, typical servings are small (5-10g), providing 3-6g of protein. Best as a supplement added to smoothies.

### 19. Buckwheat (cooked) — 3.4g protein per 100g (92 calories)
Despite its name, buckwheat is not related to wheat and is gluten-free. It is a complete protein and works well in porridge, pancakes, and as a grain bowl base.

### 20. Chia Seeds — 17g protein per 100g (486 calories)
High in protein, omega-3s, and fiber. A 20g serving (2 tablespoons) provides 3.4g of protein and nearly 7g of fiber. Make chia pudding with soy milk for a protein-rich breakfast or snack.

## Tips for Maximizing Vegan Protein

1. **Eat legumes daily.** [Lentils](/lentils), chickpeas, black beans, and edamame are the backbone of vegan protein.
2. **Choose soy milk over other plant milks.** Most almond, oat, and coconut milks have only 0.5-1g of protein per 100ml.
3. **Add hemp seeds and nutritional yeast liberally.** They boost protein in almost any dish without changing the flavor much.
4. **Use tempeh and seitan for main dishes.** They have the highest protein density among whole plant foods.
5. **Eat grains and legumes together across the day** for complete amino acid coverage.
6. **Consider a vegan protein powder** if you struggle to hit 1.6+ g/kg through food alone.

## Key Takeaways

- Vegans can easily meet protein needs by eating a variety of legumes, soy products, grains, nuts, and seeds
- The highest-protein vegan foods per 100g are seitan (25g), tempeh (19g), lentils (9g), and edamame (11g)
- Soy products (tofu, tempeh, edamame, soy milk) are complete proteins with all essential amino acids
- Aim for slightly more protein than omnivore recommendations (add 10-20%) to account for lower plant protein digestibility
- You do not need to combine proteins in every meal — variety throughout the day covers all amino acids
