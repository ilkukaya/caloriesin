## Introduction

One of the most common questions people ask when starting a health journey is: *How many calories should I eat per day?* The answer depends on several personal factors, including your age, sex, height, weight, and how active you are.

Getting your daily calorie intake right is the foundation of any nutrition plan. Eat too few calories and you risk fatigue, nutrient deficiencies, and muscle loss. Eat too many and you may gain unwanted weight over time. This guide breaks down everything you need to know about finding your ideal daily calorie target.

## How Many Calories Do You Need?

As a general starting point, most adults need somewhere between **1,600 and 3,000 calories per day** to maintain their current weight. The U.S. Dietary Guidelines provide these broad estimates:

- **Adult women:** 1,600 to 2,400 calories per day
- **Adult men:** 2,000 to 3,000 calories per day

These ranges are wide because calorie needs vary significantly from person to person. A 25-year-old man who runs five miles a day has very different energy requirements than a 60-year-old woman with a desk job.

## Factors That Affect Your Calorie Needs

Several key factors determine how many calories your body burns each day:

### Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)

Your BMR is the number of calories your body needs just to stay alive — breathing, circulating blood, and maintaining organ function. BMR accounts for roughly **60 to 75%** of your total daily energy expenditure. People with more muscle mass tend to have a higher BMR.

### Activity Level

Physical activity is the second-largest contributor to calorie burn. This includes structured exercise like running or weight training, but also everyday movement such as walking, climbing stairs, and doing household chores. More active individuals need substantially more calories.

### Age

Metabolism tends to slow gradually as you age, partly due to loss of muscle mass. This means calorie needs generally decrease with age, even if your activity level stays the same.

### Sex

On average, men have more lean muscle mass and a higher BMR than women of the same age and weight, leading to higher calorie requirements.

### Body Composition

Two people who weigh the same can have very different calorie needs. Someone with a higher proportion of lean muscle burns more calories at rest than someone with a higher proportion of body fat.

## How to Calculate Your Daily Calories

The **Mifflin-St Jeor equation** is widely regarded as one of the most accurate methods for estimating BMR. It was developed in 1990 and is recommended by the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics.

**For men:**
BMR = (10 x weight in kg) + (6.25 x height in cm) - (5 x age in years) + 5

**For women:**
BMR = (10 x weight in kg) + (6.25 x height in cm) - (5 x age in years) - 161

Once you have your BMR, multiply it by an activity factor to estimate your **Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE)**:

- **Sedentary** (little or no exercise): BMR x 1.2
- **Lightly active** (light exercise 1-3 days/week): BMR x 1.375
- **Moderately active** (moderate exercise 3-5 days/week): BMR x 1.55
- **Very active** (hard exercise 6-7 days/week): BMR x 1.725
- **Extra active** (very hard exercise or physical job): BMR x 1.9

**Example:** A 30-year-old woman who is 165 cm tall, weighs 65 kg, and exercises moderately would calculate:

- BMR = (10 x 65) + (6.25 x 165) - (5 x 30) - 161 = 650 + 1,031.25 - 150 - 161 = **1,370 calories**
- TDEE = 1,370 x 1.55 = **2,124 calories per day**

## Calorie Needs for Weight Loss

To lose weight, you need to create a **calorie deficit** — meaning you consume fewer calories than your body burns. A common and sustainable approach is to reduce your intake by **500 calories per day**, which translates to roughly one pound of fat loss per week.

Here are some important guidelines for safe calorie restriction:

- **Women** should generally not eat fewer than **1,200 calories per day** without medical supervision.
- **Men** should generally not eat fewer than **1,500 calories per day** without medical supervision.
- Aim for a gradual rate of weight loss — **0.5 to 1 kg (1 to 2 lbs) per week** is considered safe and sustainable.
- Extreme calorie restriction can backfire by slowing your metabolism, increasing hunger hormones, and promoting muscle loss.

Rather than cutting calories drastically, consider combining a moderate calorie deficit with regular physical activity. This approach preserves lean muscle mass and supports long-term weight management.

## Key Takeaways

- Daily calorie needs typically range from 1,600 to 3,000 for most adults, depending on individual factors.
- The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is one of the most reliable ways to estimate your calorie needs.
- Your BMR, activity level, age, sex, and body composition all influence how many calories you burn each day.
- For weight loss, a deficit of about 500 calories per day is a safe and effective starting point.
- Never drop below minimum recommended calorie levels without professional guidance.
