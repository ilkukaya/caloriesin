## Introduction

Fruit contains sugar — that much is undeniable. A medium [banana](/banana) has 14g of sugar, a cup of grapes has 23g, and a [mango](/mango) has over 45g. These numbers lead many people on low-carb or low-sugar diets to avoid fruit entirely. But is the sugar in fruit actually harmful? Should you limit fruit intake? And does fruit sugar behave the same way in your body as the sugar in a candy bar?

The short answer: no, fruit sugar and added sugar are not the same thing in practice. This guide explains why, ranks fruits by sugar content, and helps you determine how much fruit is right for your goals.

## Natural Sugar vs. Added Sugar: Why It Matters

The sugar in fruit is primarily fructose, the same molecule found in table sugar and high-fructose corn syrup. So why do nutrition scientists treat them differently?

**Context matters.** When you eat a whole fruit, the sugar comes packaged with:

- **Fiber** — slows sugar absorption and prevents blood sugar spikes
- **Water** — adds volume, making you feel full faster
- **Vitamins and minerals** — essential nutrients your body needs
- **Phytonutrients and antioxidants** — compounds linked to disease prevention

When you eat a candy bar, you get sugar without any of these protective factors. The sugar absorbs rapidly, spikes blood sugar, and provides zero nutritional benefit beyond calories.

**Research supports this distinction.** Large-scale studies consistently show that whole fruit consumption is associated with reduced risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and certain cancers. Added sugar consumption is associated with the opposite — increased disease risk. The fiber and water in fruit make it virtually impossible to overconsume fructose from whole fruits.

## Fruits Ranked by Sugar Content

### Lowest Sugar Fruits (Under 6g per 100g)

These fruits are the best choices for people actively limiting sugar intake:

| Fruit | Sugar per 100g | Sugar per Typical Serving |
|-------|---------------|--------------------------|
| [Avocado](/avocado) | 0.7g | Half (68g): 0.5g |
| Lemon | 2.5g | 1 lemon juice: 1.2g |
| Lime | 1.7g | 1 lime juice: 0.7g |
| Cranberries (raw) | 4.0g | 1 cup: 4.7g |
| Raspberries | 4.4g | 1 cup: 5.4g |
| [Strawberries](/strawberries) | 4.9g | 1 cup: 7.4g |
| [Watermelon](/watermelon) | 6.2g | 1 cup: 9.4g |
| Grapefruit | 6.9g | Half: 8.5g |

Berries stand out as the best everyday fruit for people watching sugar. [Strawberries](/strawberries) have just 4.9g of sugar per 100g and provide abundant vitamin C and fiber.

### Moderate Sugar Fruits (7-12g per 100g)

These fruits have moderate sugar and are fine for most people in normal portions:

| Fruit | Sugar per 100g | Sugar per Typical Serving |
|-------|---------------|--------------------------|
| Peach | 8.4g | 1 medium: 13g |
| Cantaloupe | 7.9g | 1 cup: 12.6g |
| [Blueberries](/blueberries) | 10.0g | 1 cup: 14.7g |
| Pineapple | 9.9g | 1 cup: 16.3g |
| [Apple](/apple) | 10.4g | 1 medium: 19g |
| Orange | 9.4g | 1 medium: 12g |
| Kiwi | 9.0g | 1 medium: 6.8g |
| Plum | 9.9g | 1 medium: 6.6g |
| Pear | 9.8g | 1 medium: 17g |
| Cherries | 8.5g | 1 cup: 13g |

[Apples](/apple) are frequently cited as high-sugar fruits, but a medium apple's 19g of sugar comes with 4.4g of fiber and requires significant chewing, which slows consumption and promotes satiety.

### Higher Sugar Fruits (Over 12g per 100g)

These fruits are higher in sugar and portions may matter more for people managing blood sugar:

| Fruit | Sugar per 100g | Sugar per Typical Serving |
|-------|---------------|--------------------------|
| Grapes | 16.3g | 1 cup: 23g |
| [Banana](/banana) | 12.2g | 1 medium: 14g |
| [Mango](/mango) | 13.7g | 1 whole: 46g |
| Pomegranate | 13.7g | 1/2 cup seeds: 12g |
| Figs (fresh) | 16.3g | 1 medium: 8g |

### Dried Fruits (Very High Sugar)

Drying concentrates sugar dramatically. Compare fresh vs. dried:

| Fruit | Fresh (per 100g) | Dried (per 100g) |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Grapes / Raisins | 16.3g | 59.2g |
| Apricots | 9.2g | 53.4g |
| Figs | 16.3g | 47.9g |
| Cranberries | 4.0g | 72.6g (usually sweetened) |
| Dates | 63.4g | 66.5g |
| [Mango](/mango) | 13.7g | 73.0g (usually sweetened) |

Dried fruits should be treated like candy in terms of portion control. A 50g handful of raisins contains nearly 30g of sugar.

## How Fruit Sugar Affects Blood Sugar

The glycemic index (GI) measures how quickly a food raises blood sugar. Despite containing sugar, most whole fruits have a low to moderate GI:

| Fruit | Glycemic Index | Category |
|-------|---------------|----------|
| Cherries | 22 | Low |
| Grapefruit | 25 | Low |
| [Apple](/apple) | 36 | Low |
| Pear | 38 | Low |
| [Strawberries](/strawberries) | 41 | Low |
| Orange | 43 | Low |
| [Banana](/banana) (slightly unripe) | 48 | Low |
| [Mango](/mango) | 51 | Low |
| [Banana](/banana) (ripe) | 62 | Medium |
| Pineapple | 59 | Medium |
| [Watermelon](/watermelon) | 76 | High |
| Dates | 103 | High |

The fiber in whole fruits slows sugar absorption, resulting in a gentler blood sugar response than the GI number alone might suggest. The glycemic load (which accounts for typical portion size) makes most fruits even more favorable.

## How Much Fruit Should You Eat?

### General Population
Most nutrition guidelines recommend **2-4 servings of fruit per day**. No major health organization recommends limiting whole fruit intake for healthy adults. The benefits of fruit consumption far outweigh concerns about natural sugar.

### Weight Loss
Fruit is an excellent tool for weight loss. Low-sugar fruits like berries, [watermelon](/watermelon), and citrus satisfy sweet cravings for minimal calories. Even higher-sugar fruits like [bananas](/banana) (105 calories) and [apples](/apple) (95 calories) are far better snack choices than processed alternatives.

**Practical tip:** Eat whole fruits instead of drinking fruit juice. A cup of orange juice (112 calories, 21g sugar, 0g fiber) is nutritionally inferior to an actual orange (62 calories, 12g sugar, 3g fiber).

### Diabetes or Insulin Resistance
People with diabetes can and should eat fruit, but portion control and selection matter more. Prioritize lower-sugar fruits (berries, citrus, melons), eat fruit with a protein or fat source to slow sugar absorption, and monitor blood sugar responses to find which fruits work best for you. Consult your doctor or dietitian for personalized guidance.

### Keto Diet
Most fruits exceed keto's 20-50g daily carb limit in a single serving. Exceptions include [avocado](/avocado), small portions of berries (50-75g), and lemon/lime juice for flavoring. See our [keto diet food list](/blog/keto-diet-food-list) for details.

## Key Takeaways

- Fruit sugar is not the same as added sugar — the fiber, water, and nutrients in whole fruit protect against negative effects
- The lowest-sugar fruits are avocado, berries, and citrus
- The highest-sugar fresh fruits are grapes, [mango](/mango), and figs
- Dried fruits have 3-5x the sugar concentration of fresh fruits — treat portions like candy
- Most healthy adults should eat 2-4 servings of whole fruit daily without concern
- Whole fruits are always preferable to fruit juice for weight and blood sugar management
- People with diabetes can eat fruit but should prioritize lower-sugar options and monitor blood sugar response
